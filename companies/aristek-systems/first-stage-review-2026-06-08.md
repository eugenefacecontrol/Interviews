The answer sounded uncertain: internal project, maybe like Jira, do not remember exactly what the tests were for. That is understandable historically, but in an interview it can sound like weak ownership.

Better version:

> EPAM was my entry point into automation. It was an internal product with regression automation. My responsibility was mostly maintaining existing automated tests, analyzing failures, and keeping regression checks stable. The main value from that period was building the base: how automated regression works, how to investigate failures, and how to work with an existing framework.

### DevStream / Leapwork explanation needs to be tighter

The explanation was correct but a bit scattered.

Better version:

> DevStream was the outsourcing company through which I worked on the Leapwork product. Later the cooperation became more direct, but from my perspective it was a long product journey around the same automation platform. That is why I treat it as one continuous domain experience: no-code automation, integrations, agents, test execution, and helping users automate complex systems.

### Current company/product answer was not clean enough

The legal-documents product explanation had hesitations and some wording problems.

Better version:

> Define Alley is a Microsoft Word add-in for legal professionals. It helps users work with definitions, clauses, references, comparisons, and document structure inside legal documents. My automation work covered the product area around clauses/definitions, PDF-related flows, AI-related parts, and later Playwright/TypeScript automation for the newer app version.

### AI/tools answer became too personal and too detailed

Mentioning token counts, subscriptions, and personal OpenClaw setup is interesting but too much for a recruiter/interviewer. It can distract from professional value.

Better version:

> Professionally, I use AI tools for test idea generation, test case drafts, code review, refactoring suggestions, log analysis, and creating helper scripts. At work we also experimented with agents for creating tests, reviewing/refactoring them, and generating test cases. I treat AI output as a draft: it speeds up work, but the engineer still validates the result.

### Documentation phrase should be softened

Saying "I'm not a big fan of documentation" can sound risky.

Better version:

> I prefer lean and useful documentation rather than documentation for its own sake. For onboarding and automation, documentation is important, and AI can help keep it easier to create and maintain.

### English was passable but not as crisp as your technical level

The recruiter accepted it, but for a client-facing role it is worth tightening common phrases:

- "It clicked for me" instead of "it just clicked when I..."
- "I missed the visible product feedback in QA automation" instead of searching for wording.
- "I worked with English-speaking teams/clients throughout most of my career" instead of "only anglophone".
- "I use Python as a secondary/practical automation language" instead of over-explaining that reading is not enough.

## Ready Improved Answers For Future Calls

### Why automation?

> Automation has been a consistent pattern for me, both professionally and personally. I like turning repeated work into reliable systems, whether it is test automation, scripts, CI workflows, or small tools. QA automation is especially interesting because it connects engineering with visible product quality: you can build something technical and immediately see how it protects real user flows.

### Why QA automation instead of DevOps?

> I like DevOps because it is also automation, and that experience helps me with CI, environments, logs, and infrastructure thinking. But QA automation fits me better long-term because it is closer to product behavior and user workflows. I enjoy selectors, API behavior, test design, failure analysis, and seeing the direct quality impact.

### What kind of technical leader are you?

> I am not trying to be a people manager by default. My strongest leadership is technical: onboarding, explaining systems, sharing automation practices, helping manual testers move toward automation, and improving the way the team works with tests and tools.

### How do you use AI?

> I use AI as an engineering assistant: test ideas, edge cases, test case drafts, code review, refactoring suggestions, log analysis, and helper scripts. I do not treat it as an authority. I validate the result and follow company/client privacy rules, especially around code, logs, and production-like data.

### What do you prefer in the next role?

> I prefer a role where I can use and deepen automation around C#, Python, JavaScript/TypeScript, APIs, CI/CD, and product-quality workflows. I am open on domain, but I would avoid iGaming. I am most interested in teams where automation is treated as engineering work, not only test execution.
